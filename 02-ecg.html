High-yield card read first

The 11-step read

  • Identification → Standardisation → Voltage → Axis → Rhythm → Rate → P → PR → QRS → ST → T
  • Paper speed 25 mm/s: 1 small square = 0.04 s, 1 big square = 0.2 s
  • Standardisation: 10 mm = 1 mV

Rate

  • Regular: 300 ÷ big squares between R–R, or 1500 ÷ small squares
  • Irregular: QRS count in 6 s (30 big squares) × 10
  • Six-second method is the least accurate (use for irregular rhythms)

Normal values

  • P wave: height < 2.5 mm, width < 0.10 s; upright in I & II, inverted in aVR
  • PR 0.12–0.20 s (3–5 small squares)
  • QRS < 0.12 s (< 3 small squares)
  • High voltage (LVH): S V1/V2 + R V5/V6 ≥ 35 mm
  • Intrinsic rates: SA 60–100, AV junction 40–60, ventricle 20–40

Must-know management

  • Stable torsades → IV magnesium
  • VF / pulseless VT → defibrillate (unsynchronised)
  • SVT, vagal manoeuvre failed → adenosine 6 mg IV
  • 3rd-degree / Mobitz II → pacemaker
  • Asymptomatic Mobitz I (athlete) → observe
  • AF + WPW → procainamide (avoid AV-node blockers)

Memory hooks

300-150-100-75-60-50

Rate when the next R wave lands on each successive big-square line.

Wenckebach: longer, longer, drop

"Longer, longer, longer, drop, then you have a Wenckebach."

Junction = 40–60

SA node 60–100, AV junction 40–60, ventricles 20–40 (fail-safe backups get slower as you go down).

Torsades → Mg

Twisting points → "Magnesium untwists"; think QT-prolonging drugs (methadone, ondansetron, amiodarone) and low K/Mg/Ca.

Exam traps

VF with a pulseless patient needs unsynchronised defibrillation. Synchronised cardioversion is for unstable tachycardia with a pulse (the 28% wrong answer in the CBL).
Asymptomatic Mobitz I in an athlete needs observation, not a pacemaker. Mobitz II and 3rd-degree need a pacemaker.
AF with WPW (delta wave): don't give adenosine, verapamil, digoxin or beta-blockers. They push conduction down the accessory pathway.
The six-second method is the least accurate way to count rate. Use it only for irregular rhythms.
Torsades: give magnesium even if the serum Mg is normal.
Adenosine is contraindicated in asthma (bronchospasm).

Q1Past-paper style

ST elevation in leads I and aVL. Which part of the heart is involved?

Answer C: Lateral

I and aVL look at the high lateral wall (LCx territory).

Q2Past-paper style

Intrinsic firing rate of the AV junction?

Answer B: 40–60 bpm

The conduction system is a fail-safe hierarchy: SA node 60–100, AV junction 40–60, ventricles 20–40.

Source: Slide 'Cardiac conduction system – fail-safe'; 2025-26 Quiz 1 (Cardio Q14)

Q3Past-paper style

S wave in V1 plus R wave in V5 measures 45 small squares. How do you describe the voltage?

Answer E: High voltage

The lecture criterion: (S V1 or V2) + (R V5 or V6) ≥ 35 mm = high voltage (LVH). 45 mm is well above it.

Source: Slide 'Voltage – high voltage criteria'; 2025-26 Quiz 1 (Cardio Q13)

Q4Past-paper style

Stable patient with torsades de pointes. Treatment?

Answer B: IV magnesium sulfate

IV magnesium sulfate is first-line for torsades regardless of serum magnesium; stop QT-prolonging drugs.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q16)

Q5Past-paper style

ECG shows third-degree heart block. What is the definitive treatment?

Answer D: Pacemaker placement

Complete heart block needs a permanent pacemaker. Atropine or transcutaneous pacing only bridge.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q21)

Q6Past-paper style

Asymptomatic young athlete; ECG shows second-degree AV block, Wenckebach type. Management?

Answer A: Observation

Mobitz I in a fit athlete reflects high vagal tone and is benign; reassure and observe.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q22); CBL1-W2 (athlete AV block)

Q7Past-paper style

Tachycardia lasting more than 30 seconds (wide complex) is called?

Answer A: Sustained VT

VT ≥ 30 s (or needing termination because of instability) = sustained VT.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q32)

Q8Past-paper style

Patient with occasional palpitations; ECG shows unifocal PVCs. Next step?

Answer D: Reassurance

Occasional unifocal PVCs in a structurally normal heart are benign.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q37)

Q9Past-paper style

Narrow-complex regular tachycardia; vagal manoeuvre failed. Next step?

Answer C: 6 mg adenosine IV

Stable SVT (e.g. AVNRT): vagal manoeuvres first, then rapid IV adenosine 6 mg, then 12 mg.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q38)

Q10Past-paper style

Patient with AF; a previous ECG showed delta waves, wide QRS and short PR. Treatment?

Answer E: Procainamide

Pre-excited AF (WPW): AV-nodal blockers can accelerate conduction down the accessory pathway → VF. Use procainamide (or cardioversion if unstable).

Trap: All the AV-node blockers are wrong here.

Source: 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q23)

Q11Past-paper style

Man takes his father's verapamil by mistake and develops worsening palpitations; ECG shows delta waves. Diagnosis?

Answer B: Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (bundle of Kent)

Delta wave + short PR = accessory pathway (bundle of Kent). Verapamil blocks the AV node and speeds pathway conduction.

Source: Part 1 Final recall list B

Q12Past-paper style

An ECG shows a short PR interval, a slurred upstroke of the QRS (delta wave) and a widened QRS. What is the underlying pathophysiology?

Answer A: An accessory pathway bypassing the AV node (bundle of Kent)

In WPW, an accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) pre-excites the ventricle, which shortens the PR and produces the delta wave. AV nodal re-entry causes SVT with a normal resting PR and no delta wave.

Source: 2025-26 Final 1 Paper 2 Cardio (recall)

Q13Past-paper style

A patient has palpitations, hypotension and confusion. The ECG shows a regular narrow-complex tachycardia. What is the next step?

Answer E: Synchronised DC cardioversion

A haemodynamically UNSTABLE tachyarrhythmia with a pulse needs immediate synchronised cardioversion. Adenosine or vagal manoeuvres are for stable patients, and unsynchronised shocks are for VF or pulseless VT.

Source: 2025-26 Final 1 Paper 2 Cardio (recall)

Q14Past-paper style

A patient with a history of thromboembolic stroke presents with dyspnoea and palpitations. He is haemodynamically stable. His ECG is shown. What is the next step?

Answer D: IV adenosine

The ECG shows a regular narrow-complex tachycardia with no clear P waves. In a stable patient, IV adenosine (after vagal manoeuvres) is the next step: it terminates AV-nodal re-entry or slows the AV node to unmask flutter waves. Cardioversion is kept for unstable patients.

Source: 2025-26 Final 1 Paper 2 Cardio (recall)

Q15Past-paper style

A patient with chest pain has ST elevation in leads V2, V3 and V4. Which territory is involved?

Answer B: Anterior

V2–V4 face the anterior wall (LAD territory). Inferior = II, III, aVF; lateral = I, aVL, V5–V6.

Source: IM 2023 file – Part-1 Midblock recall (Cardiology)

Q16Past-paper style

ECG changes (ST elevation) are seen in leads V5 and V6. Which coronary artery is most likely affected?

Answer C: Left circumflex artery

V5–V6 (with I and aVL) are the lateral leads, supplied mainly by the left circumflex. The RCA gives the inferior leads (II, III, aVF); the LAD gives V1–V4.

Source: IM 2023 file – Cardiology Dr Hamdy recall Q26

Q17Past-paper style

A woman collapses and is found unresponsive. The monitor shows ventricular tachycardia. What is the treatment?

Answer C: Defibrillation (unsynchronised shock)

A collapsed patient with VT is treated as pulseless VT → immediate defibrillation with CPR. Synchronised cardioversion is for unstable VT that still has a pulse.

Source: IM 2023 file – Part 1 Final recall (Cardiology)

Q18Past-paper style

A 60-year-old man has palpitations. ECG: regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150/min with a saw-tooth baseline in II, III and aVF (atrial rate about 300/min). Diagnosis?

Answer A: Atrial flutter with 2:1 block

Saw-tooth flutter waves at an atrial rate of 250–350/min with regular block (often 2:1, ventricular rate ~150) = atrial flutter. AF is irregularly irregular with no organised atrial activity.

Source: IM 2023 file – Part 1 Final recall (Cardiology, ECG shown); Dr Hamdy recall Q11; endocrine/cardio quick list ('250–350 bpm')

Q19Past-paper style

ECG shows ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF. Which wall is involved and which artery is most likely occluded?

Answer D: Inferior wall – right coronary artery

II, III and aVF look at the inferior wall, which the RCA supplies in most people (the RCA also supplies the AV node, hence heart block). I and aVL indicate a lateral (LCx) MI; V1–V4 indicate an anterior or septal (LAD) MI.

Source: 2024-25 Final P1 Paper 2 Q22 (answer key only – stem reconstructed)

Q20Past-paper style

A patient has palpitations. The ECG shows an irregularly irregular narrow-complex rhythm with no discernible P waves and a fibrillatory baseline. Diagnosis?

Answer B: Atrial fibrillation

Absent P waves with irregularly irregular R–R intervals define AF. Flutter shows regular saw-tooth waves, and MAT shows ≥3 distinct P-wave shapes.

Source: 2024-25 Final P1 Paper 2 Q24 (answer key only – ECG not available)

Q21Past-paper style

An ECG shows a repeating pattern of two normal sinus beats followed by one premature, wide, bizarre QRS complex. What is this pattern called?

Answer E: Ventricular trigeminy

A PVC after every two normal beats is trigeminy. A PVC after every normal beat is bigeminy, and two PVCs in a row form a couplet. The ECG lecture text isn't available to check against, so this was keyed on standard ECG definitions.

Source: 2024-25 Final P1 Paper 2 Q25 (answer key only – ECG not available)

Q22High-yield

Regular rhythm; R–R interval is 4 big squares. Heart rate?

Answer B: 75

Rate = 300 ÷ big squares = 300 ÷ 4 = 75 bpm (or 1500 ÷ 20 small squares).

Source: Slide 'Rate: HR = 300/RR in big squares'

Q23High-yield

Irregular rhythm with 9 QRS complexes in a 6-second strip. Rate?

Answer C: 90

Irregular rhythm: QRS in 6 s (30 big squares) × 10 = 90 bpm.

Source: Slide 'Rate (irregular)'

Q24High-yield

At standard paper speed, how long is one small square?

Answer B: 0.04 s

25 mm/s → 1 mm (small square) = 0.04 s; 1 big square (5 mm) = 0.2 s; 1500 small squares per minute.

Source: Slide 'ECG paper'

Q25High-yield

Lead I positive, aVF negative, lead II negative. The axis is?

Answer C: Left axis deviation

I positive with aVF negative suggests left axis; a negative lead II confirms true LAD (beyond −30°).

Source: Slide 'Axis'

Q26High-yield

Tall peaked P wave of 3.5 mm in lead II indicates?

Answer E: Right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale)

P amplitude > 2.5 mm = right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale). Wide notched P > 0.10 s = P mitrale.

Source: Slide 'P-wave abnormalities'

Q27High-yield

Which feature defines Mobitz II rather than Mobitz I?

Answer B: Constant PR with suddenly dropped QRS

Mobitz II: fixed PR, sudden non-conducted P. It is infranodal and can progress to complete block → pacemaker.

Source: AV block strips (standard teaching)

Q28CBL / AMBOSS style

A 71-year-old with CAD, on metoprolol, has 3 days of palpitations and near-syncope. Pulse 136 and irregular. During an episode the ECG shows sinus bradycardia with a sinus pause, then an escape rhythm. Mechanism?

Answer A: Degeneration of sinoatrial node automaticity

Sick sinus syndrome (tachy-brady): age-related SA-node fibrosis, worsened by beta-blockers. Symptomatic SSS → pacemaker.

Source: CBL1-W1 Q6

Q29CBL / AMBOSS style

A 62-year-old on methadone and ondansetron has syncope. Telemetry: polymorphic VT with a twisting QRS axis that self-terminates. Electrolytes normal. Underlying cause?

Answer D: Prolonged QT interval

Torsades de pointes from drug-induced QT prolongation (methadone, ondansetron). Treat with IV magnesium.

Source: CBL1-W5 Q6

Q30CBL / AMBOSS style

A 46-year-old woman has sudden palpitations after drinking beers. Pulse 180 regular, BP 130/82. ECG: regular narrow QRS, no visible P waves. Valsalva fails. Next step?

Answer D: IV adenosine

Stable AVNRT: after vagal manoeuvres fail, IV adenosine terminates > 80%. Cardioversion is for unstable patients.

Source: CBL1-W5 Q5

Q31CBL / AMBOSS style

A 42-year-old with an inferior STEMI loses consciousness while the ECG is recorded. The trace shows chaotic undulations with no P, QRS or T waves. Next step?

Answer A: Unsynchronised cardioversion (defibrillation)

Ventricular fibrillation → immediate defibrillation with high-quality CPR. Drugs are adjuncts only.

Trap: Synchronised cardioversion (28% chose it) needs an organised QRS to sync to.

Source: CBL2-W5 Q3

Q32CBL / AMBOSS style

Twelve hours after an inferior MI, a 65-year-old woman has dizziness and brief syncope; pulse 40. ECG: P waves at a regular rate with no relation to the QRS complexes. Diagnosis?

Answer B: Third-degree (complete) AV block

AV dissociation after inferior MI (RCA supplies the AV node in most people) = complete heart block.

Source: CBL2-W5 Q6

Q33Exam trap

Which drug is contraindicated for SVT in a patient with asthma?

Answer E: Adenosine

Adenosine can cause bronchospasm; avoid it in asthma/reactive airway disease. Use a calcium-channel blocker instead.

Source: CBL1-W5 Q5 explanation

Q34Exam trap

Which ECG finding is NOT seen in atrial fibrillation?

Answer C: Saw-tooth flutter waves at ~300/min

Saw-tooth waves belong to atrial flutter. AF has no organised atrial activity.

Source: Slide 'P-wave absent – fibrillatory, flutter'
