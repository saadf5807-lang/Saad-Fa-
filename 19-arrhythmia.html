High-yield card read first

Classify first

  • Brady < 60, tachy > 100. SVT starts at/above the AV node (narrow); VT starts below it (wide QRS)
  • Any tachycardia: stable or unstable? Unstable = chest pain, altered mental status, pulmonary oedema, hypotension, shock → synchronised DC
  • Stable: narrow/wide × regular/irregular = the 4 groups
  • Wide regular = VT until proven otherwise; never give verapamil

Bradycardia

  • Unstable → atropine 1 mg IV q3–5 min (max 3 mg)
  • Fails → transcutaneous/transvenous pacing or dopamine / adrenaline infusion → treat cause → PPM
  • Mobitz I = progressive PR, AV node, benign. Mobitz II / 3rd degree = infranodal → pacemaker
  • Tachy-brady (sick sinus) → pacemaker + rate control

SVT (AVNRT / AVRT)

  • AVNRT = dual AV-nodal pathways, 60–70% of PSVT, neck pounding, 150–250, P hidden in QRS
  • Acute: vagal → adenosine 6 mg rapid IV (→ 12 → 12) → verapamil 5 mg / β-blocker / diltiazem
  • Cure: RF ablation (> 98%). WPW → EP study + ablation
  • AVRT: orthodromic = narrow; antidromic = wide with delta waves

Atrial fibrillation

  • Unstable → DC 150 J. < 48 h → propafenone (no HF) or IV amiodarone
  • > 48 h or unknown → TEE or anticoagulate first, and after (warfarin INR 2–3)
  • Rate: β-blocker / non-DHP CCB → digoxin → amiodarone → ablate & pace
  • CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥ 2 ♂ / ≥ 3 ♀, valvular AF or HCM → anticoagulate; weigh HAS-BLED
  • New AF → check TSH

Memory hooks

"ABCD" in WPW + AF

Avoid Adenosine, Beta-blockers, Calcium-channel blockers, Digoxin. Give procainamide or shock.

150 / 200 J

Slide numbers: AF → 150 J, VT → 200 J synchronised. VF = unsynchronised.

Wenckebach walks, Mobitz II drops

PR walks longer in Mobitz I; in Mobitz II it stays fixed and a beat suddenly drops → pace.

48-hour rule

AF > 48 h (or unknown) = clot until proven otherwise → TEE or anticoagulate first.

Exam traps

Verapamil (or any AV-nodal blocker) in a wide-complex tachycardia or pre-excited AF can precipitate VF.
Asystole is never shocked: CPR + adrenaline.
Unknown-duration AF counts as > 48 h.
Valvular AF (moderate–severe MS, mechanical valve) → warfarin regardless of score; DOACs are for non-valvular AF.
A high HAS-BLED means fix the bleeding risks and monitor, not automatically withhold anticoagulation.
Propafenone (class IC) is contraindicated with HF / structural heart disease; use amiodarone.
Lecture vs textbook · Slide 84 (older algorithm) says "consider amiodarone" for pre-excited AF; the WPW slide (77) says procainamide or cardioversion. Answer procainamide.
Lecture vs textbook · Slide 60 amiodarone load for AF is written as 300 mg over 40 min then 1800 mg/24 h; slide 84 uses 300 mg then 900 mg/24 h. Quote the number for the setting asked.

Q1Past-paper styleHigh-yield

A 64-year-old man has palpitations and is found to have atrial fibrillation. He had a TIA last year. What is the next step in management?

Answer A: Start apixaban

CHA₂DS₂-VASc = age 65–74 (1) + TIA (2) = 3 → long-term oral anticoagulation. Non-valvular AF → a DOAC such as apixaban. Aspirin is not adequate stroke prevention.

Source: Part 1 Final recall (MI 1st-semester file, Cardiology); slides 65–66

Q2Past-paper style

A 60-year-old man has new-onset atrial fibrillation that has been rate-controlled. Which test should be obtained next?

Answer A: TSH

Thyrotoxicosis is a reversible precipitant of AF, so every new AF needs TSH (slide 68: identify and treat reversible precipitants).

Source: Part 1 Final recall (MI 1st-semester file, Cardiology)

Q3Past-paper style

A 54-year-old man is found incidentally to have atrial fibrillation with a fast ventricular rate. If it is left untreated, which complication of the persistent tachycardia is he at risk of?

Answer D: Dilated (tachycardia-induced) cardiomyopathy

Chronic uncontrolled tachycardia dilates and weakens the LV (tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy), which usually reverses with rate control. Stroke is the other big risk.

Source: Part 1 Final recall (MI 1st-semester file, Cardiology)

Q4Past-paper styleHigh-yield

A patient with symptomatic bradycardia (HR 34, BP 78/45, confused) does not respond to atropine. The pacing pads are not yet available. Which drug infusion is indicated?

Answer D: Dopamine

Bradycardia algorithm: atropine fails → temporising measures: transcutaneous/transvenous pacing OR dopamine / adrenaline infusion, then treat the cause and arrange a pacemaker.

Source: 2024-25 Final 1 Paper 2 Q29 (answer key "Dopamine" only; stem reconstructed); slide 23

Q5Past-paper style

In the emergency department a patient collapses; the monitor shows ventricular fibrillation and there is no pulse. CPR has started. What is the most important next step?

Answer E: Deliver an unsynchronised shock

VF/pulseless VT = shockable rhythm → immediate unsynchronised defibrillation with high-quality CPR. Drugs (adrenaline, amiodarone) are adjuncts after shocks. Synchronisation needs an R wave.

Source: 2024-25 Final 1 Paper 2 Q36 (answer key "Deliver shock" only; stem reconstructed); slide 82

Q6CBL / AMBOSS styleExam trap

A 16-year-old had a WPW pattern on his ECG after cardioversion. Now he has palpitations, HR 220, BP 112/84, alert. ECG: regular wide monomorphic QRS with short PR. What is the next step?

Answer B: IV procainamide

Stable pre-excited (antidromic) tachycardia in WPW: avoid AV-nodal blockers and vagal manoeuvres; rhythm control with procainamide (or cardioversion) is safest. He is stable, so chemical first. Mg is for torsades.

Trap: Adenosine, verapamil and β-blockers block the AV node and can push impulses down the accessory pathway → VF.

Source: Lecture opening case (slides 5–6); slide 77

Q7High-yield

A 72-year-old woman with hypertension and type 2 diabetes has new non-valvular AF. What is her CHA₂DS₂-VASc score?

Answer E: 4

HTN 1 + DM 1 + age 65–74 1 + female 1 = 4 → long-term anticoagulation (women ≥ 3).

Source: Slide 65 'CHA₂DS₂-VASc'; slide 66

Q8Exam trap

A 58-year-old woman has paroxysmal non-valvular AF and no other medical problems. What is the anticoagulation recommendation?

Answer E: Not routinely recommended (score 1 from female sex only)

Female sex alone scores 1 = low risk for a woman (thresholds are ≥ 3 for women). Anticoagulation is not routinely recommended.

Source: Slide 66 'Who to anticoagulate'

Q9CBL / AMBOSS styleHigh-yield

A stable 70-year-old has AF of unknown duration, rate 110. Elective cardioversion is planned. What must be done first?

Answer C: TEE to exclude LA thrombus (or ≥ 3 weeks anticoagulation) before cardioversion

Unknown duration = treat as > 48 h → exclude LA appendage thrombus by TEE and/or anticoagulate before reversion, and continue anticoagulation after (atrial stunning).

Source: Slides 61–62 'AF > 48 h'

Q10Past-paper styleHigh-yield

A patient with AF has HR 160, BP 80/50 and pulmonary oedema. What is the treatment?

Answer B: Synchronised DC cardioversion (150 J)

Haemodynamically unstable AF → synchronised DC shock; the slide gives 150 J. Unsynchronised shocks are for VF/pulseless VT.

Source: Slide 60 'Rhythm control – unstable'

Q11CBL / AMBOSS styleExam trap

A 68-year-old with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (EF 30%) has AF that started 10 hours ago. He is stable. Which drug is appropriate for chemical cardioversion?

Answer C: IV amiodarone

Recent-onset (< 48 h) AF can be cardioverted chemically. Propafenone and flecainide (class IC) are contraindicated with HF or impaired LV function → IV amiodarone.

Source: Slide 60 'Medical reversion: propafenone only if no CHF'

Q12Past-paper style

First-line drugs for ventricular rate control in AF?

Answer A: β-blockers or non-dihydropyridine CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem)

Rate control: β-blocker or non-DHP CCB first, digoxin second, amiodarone third; AV-node ablation + pacemaker if drugs fail.

Source: Slide 64 'Rate control'

Q13Past-paper style

A patient has frequent symptomatic recurrences of paroxysmal AF despite antiarrhythmic drugs. What is the interventional treatment?

Answer C: Pulmonary vein isolation (catheter ablation)

The triggers of AF sit around the pulmonary vein ostia; catheter ablation isolating them (PVI) is the slide answer for frequent recurrence.

Source: Slide 63 'Pulmonary vein isolation'

Q14Past-paper styleHigh-yield

What is the mechanism of AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT)?

Answer E: Re-entry using fast and slow pathways within the AV node

AVNRT uses dual AV-nodal pathways: a premature beat goes down the slow pathway (fast is refractory) and back up the fast one. Accessory pathway = AVRT; tricuspid annulus circuit = typical flutter.

Source: Slides 47–49 'AVNRT'

Q15Past-paper style

The most common cause of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia is?

Answer C: AVNRT

AVNRT accounts for 60–70% of PSVTs, usually in a structurally normal heart.

Source: Slide 49 'AVNRT'

Q16CBL / AMBOSS style

A 27-year-old woman has had six episodes of documented AVNRT this year, each needing adenosine in the ED. What is the best long-term treatment?

Answer E: Radiofrequency catheter ablation

Slide: first-line long-term treatment is RF ablation (> 98% cure, < 1% complications). β-blocker, diltiazem or digoxin are alternatives; flecainide/propafenone second line.

Source: Slide 52 'Long-term treatment'; slide 58

Q17High-yield

Vagal manoeuvres fail in a stable narrow regular tachycardia. Adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push does not terminate it. What next?

Answer E: Adenosine 12 mg rapid IV push

Slide 84: adenosine 6 mg rapid IV, then 12 mg, then a further 12 mg. Rapid push with a flush (short half-life). DC only if unstable.

Source: Slide 84 'Narrow regular'

Q18Exam trap

In atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia, which statement is correct?

Answer E: Orthodromic AVRT (down the AV node, up the accessory pathway) gives a narrow QRS

Orthodromic = antegrade through the normal system → narrow. Antidromic = down the accessory pathway → wide, pre-excited. A concealed (retrograde-only) pathway gives a normal resting ECG.

Source: Slide 50 'AVRT'

Q19Exam trapHigh-yield

A patient with known WPW develops irregular wide-complex tachycardia (pre-excited AF). Which drug is CONTRAINDICATED?

Answer E: Verapamil

All AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, β-blockers, verapamil/diltiazem, digoxin) are contraindicated in AF with an accessory pathway; they favour conduction down the pathway → VF. Rhythm control (procainamide or DC) is safest.

Source: Slides 74–77 'AF + WPW'

Q20Exam trap

A patient with torsades de pointes from acquired long QT (on haloperidol, K⁺ 3.0) is stable after IV magnesium. Which drug should be AVOIDED?

Answer E: IV amiodarone

Amiodarone (class III) prolongs QT and can worsen torsades. Correct K⁺/Mg²⁺, stop QT-prolonging drugs, overdrive pacing/isoprenaline for recurrences.

Source: Slide 72 'Torsades'; slide 81 'pro-arrhythmia'

Q21Past-paper styleHigh-yield

A 70-year-old has dizziness. ECG: constant PR interval with intermittent non-conducted P waves (Mobitz II). What is the definitive treatment?

Answer C: Permanent pacemaker

Mobitz II is infranodal and progresses to complete heart block → permanent pacemaker. Mobitz I (Wenckebach) is usually benign.

Source: Slides 20–21 'AV blocks'

Q22High-yield

Symptomatic bradycardia: what is the first drug and its dosing per the lecture?

Answer C: Atropine 1 mg IV, repeat every 3–5 min to a maximum of 3 mg

Slide 23: atropine 1 mg IV q3–5 min, max 3 mg; if ineffective → pacing or dopamine/adrenaline infusion.

Source: Slide 23 'Bradycardia algorithm'

Q23Exam trap

A heart-transplant recipient has symptomatic bradycardia with hypotension. Why is atropine likely to fail?

Answer B: The transplanted heart is denervated (no vagal input)

Atropine works by blocking vagal tone; a denervated transplanted heart has none, so go to pacing (or adrenaline/isoprenaline). Atropine is also often ineffective in infranodal (wide escape) block.

Source: Slide 23; standard teaching

Q24Exam trap

A patient in cardiac arrest has asystole on the monitor. What is the correct management?

Answer B: CPR and adrenaline; do not shock

Slide 82: asystole cannot be restarted by a defibrillator → CPR + adrenaline and look for reversible causes.

Source: Slide 82 'Cardioversion'

Q25CBL / AMBOSS style

A 66-year-old with old anterior MI and EF 30% has sustained monomorphic VT at 170/min. BP 118/76, alert. After the episode is terminated, what long-term therapy reduces sudden death?

Answer B: Amiodarone plus an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD)

Slide 69: VT in complicated IHD → amiodarone + ICD (RF ablation has a lower success rate here). Flecainide raises mortality after MI (CAST); verapamil is contraindicated in VT.

Source: Slide 69 'VT management'

Q26Past-paper style

According to the lecture, which antiarrhythmic is used for VT complicating an acute myocardial infarction?

Answer C: Lidocaine

Slide 69: complicated acute MI → lidocaine (class IB, acts on ischaemic tissue). Unstable VT → DC 200 J.

Source: Slide 69 'VT management'

Q27CBL / AMBOSS style

A 22-year-old athlete with a structurally normal heart on echo and MRI has recurrent monomorphic VT from the right ventricular outflow tract. Best definitive treatment?

Answer B: Radiofrequency ablation

Slide 69: VT in a normal (young) heart → RF ablation, usually curative for idiopathic RVOT/fascicular VT.

Source: Slide 69 'VT – normal heart'

Q28CBL / AMBOSS style

A 74-year-old with a COPD exacerbation has an irregular narrow tachycardia at 120 with at least three different P-wave morphologies. What is the best management?

Answer A: Treat the COPD exacerbation and hypoxia

Multifocal atrial tachycardia (≥ 3 P morphologies) is driven by COPD/hypoxia (and theophylline). Treat the underlying cause; it does not respond to cardioversion.

Source: Slide 29 'MAT'

Q29Exam trap

Before carotid sinus massage for a stable SVT, which finding is a contraindication?

Answer C: A carotid bruit or TIA last month

The slide flags "precautions": carotid massage can dislodge plaque → stroke. Avoid with a bruit or recent TIA/stroke; never massage both sides together.

Source: Slides 51, 79–80 'Vagal manoeuvres'

Q30Past-paper style

Amiodarone and sotalol belong to which Vaughan Williams class?

Answer D: Class III (K⁺ channel blockers)

Class I = Na⁺ blockers (IA quinidine/procainamide/disopyramide; IB lidocaine/mexiletine; IC flecainide/propafenone), II = β-blockers, III = K⁺ blockers (amiodarone, sotalol), IV = verapamil/diltiazem.

Source: Slide 81 'Antiarrhythmic classes'

Q31Exam trap

A patient with rheumatic moderate mitral stenosis develops AF. Which anticoagulant is appropriate?

Answer B: Warfarin (INR 2–3) regardless of CHA₂DS₂-VASc

Valvular AF (moderate–severe MS or a mechanical valve) is high risk → anticoagulate regardless of score, and DOACs are not used in this group.

Source: Slide 66 'High risk: valvular disease'

Q32CBL / AMBOSS style

A 45-year-old has frequent symptomatic unifocal PVCs (bigeminy on Holter) with a structurally normal heart. Electrolytes and TSH are normal. Treatment?

Answer E: β-blocker

Most PVCs need no treatment; frequent and significantly symptomatic ones are treated with β-blockers after treating any underlying cause.

Source: Slide 34 'PVC treatment'

Q33High-yield

A 79-year-old has tachy-brady syndrome: runs of AF at 150 followed by 5-second pauses with dizziness. What is the management?

Answer C: Permanent pacemaker, then rate-control drugs

Tachy-brady is a form of sick sinus syndrome. Rate-slowing drugs would worsen the pauses, so pace first and then control the rate.

Source: Slide 18 'Tachycardia–bradycardia syndrome'
