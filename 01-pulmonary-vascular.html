Pulmonary Vascular Disease (PE, DVT, Pulmonary Hypertension)

High-yield card read first

Pulmonary embolism

  • Most emboli come from proximal leg DVT (iliac, femoral, popliteal)
  • Commonest symptom: sudden dyspnoea; commonest sign: tachypnoea / tachycardia
  • Commonest ECG finding: sinus tachycardia (S1Q3T3 is specific but uncommon)
  • CXR is usually normal (Hampton hump, Westermark sign are rare)
  • ABG: hypoxaemia, respiratory alkalosis, ↑ A–a gradient

Diagnosis

  • Stable patient → estimate pre-test probability (Wells)
  • Low / intermediate → D-dimer; normal D-dimer rules PE out
  • High (Wells > 4) → straight to CTPA (sensitivity > 90%)
  • CTPA not possible (renal failure, contrast allergy) → V/Q scan
  • Pregnancy: no Wells; CXR first → normal CXR = V/Q, abnormal = CTPA

Treatment

  • Stable: anticoagulate (heparin → DOAC or warfarin)
  • Unstable (persistent hypotension) or RV failure → thrombolysis (alteplase)
  • Anticoagulation contraindicated → IVC filter
  • Pregnancy: LMWH (warfarin is teratogenic)
  • Massive PE: treat before imaging confirms it

Pulmonary hypertension

  • Definition: mean PAP > 20 mmHg at rest
  • Signs: loud P2, RV heave, right heart failure (JVD, hepatomegaly, oedema)
  • ECG: RVH + right axis deviation; CXR: enlarged pulmonary arteries
  • Echo is the first test; right-heart catheterisation confirms
  • O₂ if PaO₂ < 60 mmHg; vasoactive drugs only for Group 1 PAH
Lecture vs textbook · The slide on low pre-test probability says "PE rule-out criteria based on Wells score = 0". The standard tool is PERC (8 criteria). The idea is the same: low probability + all PERC negative = no D-dimer needed.

Memory hooks

PE is FATAL

Emboli types: Fat, Air, Thrombus, Amniotic fluid; thrombus is by far the commonest.

Virchow = SHE

Stasis, Hypercoagulability, Endothelial injury.

CXR normal + pregnant → V/Q

"Clean film, V/Q." Abnormal film → CT.

PH signs: Loud P2, Heave, JVD

Right heart under pressure: a loud pulmonic S2, an RV heave, then right heart failure.

Exam traps

S1Q3T3 is the famous ECG sign, but the most common ECG finding in PE is sinus tachycardia.
Do not calculate Wells in a pregnant patient. Past paper (2025-26): pregnant, DVT, dyspnoea → V/Q scan.
A normal CXR does not rule out PE. It is usually normal.
D-dimer is only useful to rule out PE in low/intermediate probability. In high probability skip it and go to CTPA.
Unstable patient: don't send them to CT first. Start treatment (thrombolysis) based on bedside echo.
Confirm pulmonary hypertension with right-heart catheterisation, not echo. Echo is the first test.

Q1Past-paper style

Pregnant woman with DVT, dyspnoea and HR 110. What is the most appropriate initial investigation for PE?

Answer E: V/Q scan

Wells is not validated in pregnancy and CT exposes the breast to radiation. Per the lecture: CXR first, and if it is normal a V/Q scan. This was the keyed answer in the 2025-26 Quiz 1.

Trap: Picking D-dimer: it is physiologically raised in pregnancy.

Source: Slide 'Diagnostics in pregnancy'; 2025-26 Quiz 1 Q1 (Pulm)

Q2Past-paper style

Patient with symptoms of PE and tachycardia; Wells score = 4.5. Next step?

Answer A: CT pulmonary angiography

Wells > 4 = PE likely (high probability) → confirmatory imaging with CTPA. D-dimer is for low/intermediate probability.

Source: Slide 'Management of stable patients'; 2023 Quiz 1 (Pulm Q3)

Q3Past-paper style

Most common ECG finding in pulmonary embolism?

Answer B: Sinus tachycardia

Sinus tachycardia is the most frequent ECG finding. S1Q3T3 and RBBB reflect acute RV strain and are specific but uncommon.

Trap: S1Q3T3 is the famous one, not the common one.

Source: 2023 Final P1 (Resp Q9)

Q4Past-paper style

Components of Virchow's triad?

Answer C: Stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury

Virchow's triad = stasis of blood flow, hypercoagulability and endothelial (vessel wall) injury. Option B is Beck's triad (tamponade).

Source: Slide 'Virchow's triad'; 2023 Final P2 (Cardio Q33)

Q5Past-paper style

Right axis deviation and a right ventricular heave. Most likely diagnosis?

Answer D: Pulmonary arterial hypertension

RV pressure overload from pulmonary hypertension produces RVH on ECG (right axis deviation) and a parasternal RV heave.

Source: Slide 'PH clinical features / ECG'; 2023 Quiz 1 (Pulm Q11)

Q6Past-paper style

Patient with features of pulmonary hypertension and RVH. Which test confirms the diagnosis?

Answer E: Right-heart catheterisation

Echo is the initial screening test, but right-heart catheterisation (mean PAP > 20 mmHg) is required for confirmation.

Trap: Echo is first; catheter is confirmatory.

Source: Slide 'PH diagnostics'; 2023 Final P1 (Resp Q8)

Q7Past-paper style

CT shows a filling defect in the pulmonary artery. The patient is hypotensive and unstable with a history of DVT. Treatment?

Answer D: Alteplase

Massive PE with persistent hypotension → systemic thrombolysis (alteplase) if no contraindication.

Source: Slide 'Management of unstable patients'; 2023 Final P1 (Resp Q22)

Q8Past-paper style

Patient with previous DVT and PE; Wells > 4; vitally stable, PE confirmed. Next step in management?

Answer C: IV unfractionated heparin (anticoagulation)

Stable PE is treated with anticoagulation. Thrombolysis is reserved for haemodynamic instability or RV failure.

Source: 2023 Final P1 (Resp Q25)

Q9Past-paper style

A young patient has had recurrent deep vein thromboses with no obvious provoking factor. Which underlying cause is most likely?

Answer A: Protein S deficiency (inherited thrombophilia)

The PE/DVT slide lists hereditary hypercoagulable states (factor V Leiden, protein C and S deficiency, antithrombin III deficiency) as risk factors. The other options are bleeding disorders, not causes of thrombosis.

Source: IM 2023 file – 2023 Final Part 2 Paper 1 (Haem Q16, answer protein S) and Haematology Dr Roshdi recall Q13 (answer protein C)

Q10High-yield

Mean pulmonary artery pressure that defines pulmonary hypertension at rest?

Answer B: > 20 mmHg

The lecture definition is mPAP > 20 mmHg at rest (older texts used ≥ 25).

Source: Slide 'PH definition'

Q11High-yield

Where do most pulmonary emboli originate?

Answer B: Deep veins of the leg above the knee (iliac, femoral, popliteal)

Most PEs arise from proximal deep vein thrombosis of the lower limb: iliac, femoral and popliteal veins.

Source: Slide 'PE etiology'

Q12High-yield

When is a V/Q scan preferred over CTPA in a non-pregnant patient?

Answer A: Severe chronic kidney disease

CTPA needs iodinated contrast. The lecture lists previous anaphylaxis, AKI, severe CKD and hyperthyroidism as reasons to use V/Q instead.

Source: Slide 'V/Q scan'

Q13High-yield

Low-to-intermediate probability of PE and a normal D-dimer. Next step?

Answer C: No further testing for PE; consider other diagnoses

D-dimer has high sensitivity: a normal result excludes PE when pre-test probability is not high.

Source: Slide 'Management of stable patients'

Q14High-yield

Patient with acute DVT has active GI bleeding. How do you prevent PE?

Answer D: Inferior vena cava filter

If anticoagulation is contraindicated, an IVC filter is placed to stop emboli reaching the lungs.

Source: Slide 'DVT management'

Q15High-yield

Pregnant woman with confirmed PE. Anticoagulant of choice?

Answer E: Low-molecular-weight heparin

LMWH does not cross the placenta. Warfarin is teratogenic and DOACs are avoided in pregnancy.

Source: Standard guideline (not explicit on slides)

Q16High-yield

DVT Wells score < 1 in a patient with a swollen leg. First test?

Answer B: D-dimer

Per the lecture: DVT Wells > 1 → duplex US; Wells < 1 → D-dimer first.

Source: Slide 'DVT diagnostics'

Q17High-yield

Which finding on examination suggests pulmonary hypertension?

Answer C: Loud P2

Raised pulmonary artery pressure makes the pulmonic component of S2 loud; an RV heave and right heart failure follow.

Source: Slide 'PH clinical features'

Q18CBL / AMBOSS style

Two days after a left total hip replacement, a 68-year-old man develops sudden dyspnoea and chest pain. HR 110, RR 30, BP 106/70, SpO₂ 89% on air. The right leg is swollen with calf pain on dorsiflexion. Creatinine 1.2 mg/dL. ECG: sinus tachycardia, right axis deviation. In addition to IV anticoagulation, what is the most appropriate next step?

Answer E: Spiral CT pulmonary angiography

He is haemodynamically stable (SBP > 90) with high probability (recent surgery, DVT signs, tachycardia). Confirm with CTPA; renal function allows contrast. D-dimer is useless at high probability and after surgery. Alteplase is for hypotension.

Source: Lecture Case Scenario 1

Q19CBL / AMBOSS style

A 67-year-old woman on paclitaxel for ovarian cancer has had 2 PEs in the past year. She now has 3 months of leg swelling, exertional dyspnoea, ascites, raised JVP and mild icterus. Lungs are clear. Most likely explanation?

Answer A: Right ventricular failure

Recurrent PE → chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (Group 4) → right ventricular failure: raised JVP, congestive hepatopathy, ascites and oedema. Budd-Chiari would not raise the JVP.

Trap: Raised JVP points to the heart, not the liver.

Source: Lecture Case Scenario 2

Q20CBL / AMBOSS style

A 68-year-old man with COPD (48 pack-years) has 4 months of fatigue, leg swelling and exertional dyspnoea. JVP raised, loud P2, bilateral pitting oedema. PaO₂ on air is 52 mmHg. Which intervention improves survival?

Answer D: Long-term oxygen therapy

Cor pulmonale from COPD (Group 3 PH). Long-term O₂ for PaO₂ < 60 mmHg (lecture) reduces hypoxic vasoconstriction and is the one therapy that improves survival. Vasodilators are for Group 1 PAH.

Source: CBL1-W2 (cor pulmonale case); PH management slide

Q21CBL / AMBOSS style

A 32-year-old woman on oral contraceptives returns from a 14-hour flight with sudden pleuritic chest pain and haemoptysis. HR 115, BP 92/60 then 78/50 despite fluids. Bedside echo shows a dilated, hypokinetic RV. What now?

Answer B: Systemic thrombolysis

Massive PE with persistent hypotension and RV dysfunction: treat before confirmatory imaging, with thrombolysis if no contraindication.

Trap: Sending an unstable patient to CT.

Source: Slide 'Unstable patients'

Q22Exam trap

A 55-year-old with sudden dyspnoea has a normal chest X-ray. Which statement is correct?

Answer A: CXR is usually normal in PE; Hampton hump and Westermark sign are rare

The CXR is usually normal in PE; its main role is to find other diagnoses. The classic signs are rare.

Source: Slide 'PE diagnostics – CXR'

Q23Exam trap

Which of the following is a cause of Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension (not secondary PH)?

Answer C: Methamphetamine use

Drug-induced PAH (methamphetamine, sympathomimetic appetite suppressants) is Group 1. COPD/OSA = Group 3, mitral stenosis = Group 2, CTEPH = Group 4.

Source: Slide 'PH etiology'

Q24Exam trap

Which is NOT a common feature of PE?

Answer E: Bradycardia

PE causes tachycardia, not bradycardia. Common features: acute dyspnoea, tachycardia, tachypnoea, pleuritic chest pain, cough and haemoptysis.

Source: Slide 'PE symptoms'
